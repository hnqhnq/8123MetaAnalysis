## 1. 5A’s — steps in the EBM process

The five steps are a cycle. Any step can be repeated.

| Step | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Assess** | Identify the clinical problem while caring for this patient |
| **Ask** | Turn it into a well-built clinical question |
| **Acquire** | Choose the right source and search |
| **Appraise** | Check validity and applicability |
| **Apply** | Combine the evidence with clinical expertise and the patient’s preferences, then use it in practice |

Two further steps make the 7A cycle: **Analyze outcomes** (did the evidence-based action work in practice?) and **Add knowledge** (turn this case into knowledge for the next patient). The order is assess → ask → acquire → appraise → apply → analyze → add knowledge.

A parallel five-principle list on the same slides is: build and classify the question (therapy, diagnosis, aetiology, prognosis, prevention, or cost); find the evidence; critically appraise validity and usefulness; integrate the evidence with patient factors; evaluate the whole process.

## 2. PICOS model

PICOS turns a foreground question into searchable parts. PICO clarifies the question, marks the information needed, and supplies the search terms. S states which study design can answer it.

| Letter | Meaning | What to specify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **P** | Patient, population, or problem | The features that make the group like the patient in front of you |
| **I** | Intervention, exposure, or prognostic factor | The drug, test, exposure, or factor under study |
| **C** | Comparison | The main alternative, often usual care or no intervention |
| **O** | Outcome | The benefit or harm to be measured, stated specifically |
| **S** | Study design | The design the question requires, such as an RCT for a treatment question |

T (time frame, or sometimes study type) is an optional extension. Qualitative questions in this course use PICo or SPIDER instead of PICOS.

Example from the slides. For “Does metformin have a role in adults with type 2 diabetes?”: P = adults with type 2 diabetes; I = metformin; C = no metformin, or lifestyle change alone; O = improved glycaemic control; S = a randomized trial for a treatment question.

If the comparison is not stated in the source question, record it as not stated. Do not invent a placebo. In a prognosis question, the I cell holds the exposure or index event, not a treatment. The seizure example puts “one unexplained seizure” in I and “no seizure” in C.

## 3. Categories of evidence

The slide that names four categories is:

| Category | What it is | Examples on the slide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Studies** | Unfiltered original research | MEDLINE, PubMed |
| **Summaries** | Systematic reviews | Cochrane |
| **Synopses** | Pre-appraised journal summaries | ACP Journal Club |
| **Systems** | Evidence built into a decision system | Clinical Evidence, UpToDate |

On that slide, “Summaries” means systematic reviews. On the Haynes 5S slide, the same word means an evidence-based textbook, and systematic reviews are called **Syntheses**.

Haynes 5S, from the top down (Haynes, *ACP Journal Club* 2006):

| Level | Content | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Systems | Computerized decision support | CDSS |
| Summaries | Evidence-based textbooks | UpToDate, DynaMed, ACP PIER, BMJ Clinical Evidence |
| Synopses | Structured abstracts of single studies or reviews | *EBM*, *EBN*, *EBMH*, ACP Journal Club |
| Syntheses | Systematic reviews | Cochrane reviews |
| Studies | Original articles | BMJUpdates, PubMed, Clinical Queries |

Search the upper levels first. The four levels above Studies are the ones the lecture treats as the first place to look in practice.

A third classification on the same lecture, by how far the literature has been processed:

| Level | Definition on that slide | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Original research in peer-reviewed journals | An RCT paper |
| Secondary | Indexes used to locate published literature | MEDLINE / PubMed, Cochrane Library, Ovid, Embase |
| Tertiary | Knowledge accepted as a community standard | *Harrison’s*, the Physicians’ Desk Reference |

The design pyramid, from the bottom up, is background and expert opinion, a single case report, a case series, a cohort, a non-randomized trial, an RCT, critically appraised topics and evidence-based guidelines, a systematic review, and a meta-analysis. In this course, systematic reviews and meta-analyses sit at the top. A poor review can still be less trustworthy than a well-done RCT; risk of bias and GRADE make that judgement.

## 4. Benefits of adopting EBM

The lecture’s summary of the benefits is:

1. **Fewer errors and better patient care.**
2. **Lower treatment costs**, by dropping interventions that do not work and avoiding waste.
3. **Knowledge moves forward**, and practice keeps up with the science.

EBM also supplies a chance to change practice and a chance of better treatment. It works when clinicians can reach online evidence, understand study design and basic statistics, and treat up-to-date knowledge as part of clinical competence.

Sackett’s definition is the frame for these benefits: the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the individual patient. Best external evidence, clinical expertise, and the patient’s values and circumstances are used together.

## 5. Key steps for a systematic review and meta-analysis

The course writes the same pipeline twice. The shorter list is the one drawn as eight boxes. The longer list is the written 12-step guide.

**Eight steps**

1. Define the question and the eligibility criteria in PICOS.
2. Search, with a documented strategy, in electronic and non-electronic sources.
3. Screen titles and abstracts after duplicate removal.
4. Select studies against the full eligibility criteria.
5. Extract data: PICO, study characteristics, risk of bias, and the numbers needed for analysis.
6. Analyse the data. Meta-analysis is this step when pooling is appropriate.
7. Report the review.
8. Update the review.

The illustrated version of the same pipeline names GRADE and the conclusions inside the last steps: after extraction, undertake the meta-analysis, assess certainty with GRADE, and draw conclusions.

**Twelve steps**

1. Form the question. It should be feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, and relevant (FINER). Use PICO for a quantitative review and SPIDER for a qualitative or mixed-methods review.
2. Run a preliminary search, to see whether the review already exists and how many studies are likely.
3. Set inclusion and exclusion criteria before the full search.
4. Build the search strategy.
5. Search the databases. The AMSTAR line in Lesson 2 says at least two databases; Lesson 3’s classroom requirement is at least three.
6. Write and register the protocol, early, on PROSPERO or a registry recommended by Cochrane or Campbell.
7. Screen titles and abstracts, with two or three reviewers.
8. Retrieve and screen full texts, and record reasons for exclusion.
9. Search by hand: reference lists, authors, experts, related articles, and citing articles.
10. Extract data and appraise quality or risk of bias. Pilot the form first.
11. Analyse. Quantitative synthesis is the meta-analysis; if studies cannot be pooled, present a qualitative synthesis in tables or text.
12. Write, revise, and submit. Report with PRISMA: the 27-item checklist and the flow diagram.

Before the work starts, the course also requires a team of at least three people (a content expert, a review-methods expert, and a statistician) and prospective registration.

A systematic review can be valid without a meta-analysis. Pooling needs at least two studies that are similar enough. The pooled estimate is the meta-analysis, usually shown on a forest plot.

## 6. Types and functions of Boolean logic terms

Boolean operators set the relationship between search concepts.

| Operator | Function | Effect on the set | Example from the slides |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **AND** | Keep records that contain every listed term | Narrows | “child obesity” AND Maryland |
| **OR** | Keep records that contain any listed term | Broadens | “child obesity” OR “overweight children” |
| **NOT** | Remove records that contain the term after NOT | Narrows | obesity AND children NOT adults |

Use **OR inside one concept** (synonyms and subject headings together) and **AND between concepts**. Parentheses set the order; this is nesting. Example: `(children AND teenagers) AND (ADHD OR hyperactivity)`. Quotation marks keep a phrase in order, as in “eating disorder”.

`NOT` also drops records that mention both terms. `children NOT adults` removes papers that discuss children and adults together, including some that belong in the review.

## 7. GRADE: 3 upward factors and 5 downward factors

GRADE rates the certainty of a body of evidence: how much confidence one can place in an estimate of effect. Randomized trials start at **high**. Other designs start at **low**. The two designs are rated separately. Certainty is then moved down or up.

The four levels are high (++++), moderate (+++), low (++), and very low (+).

**Five factors that can lower certainty.** Each can be −1 (serious) or −2 (very serious).

| Factor | What it captures |
| --- | --- |
| Risk of bias | Limitations in design or conduct that matter for this outcome |
| Inconsistency | Unexplained differences in the direction or size of effects, including a large I² |
| Indirectness | Populations, interventions, comparisons, or outcomes that differ from the question, including surrogate outcomes and a lack of direct comparisons |
| Imprecision | Few participants, few events, or a confidence interval too wide to be informative, such as one that includes RR = 1 |
| Publication bias | Selective publication that systematically pushes the estimate up or down. Clues include small studies, industry funding, and an asymmetric funnel plot |

**Three factors that can raise certainty.** These are used mainly for evidence that started low.

| Factor | What it captures |
| --- | --- |
| Large effect | A large effect is less likely to be entirely spurious. On the standard GRADE rule, a risk ratio of at least 2 or at most 0.5 raises certainty by one level, and a risk ratio of at least 5 or at most 0.2 raises it by two |
| Dose–response | A dose–response gradient is observed (+1 on the slide) |
| Direction of residual confounding | All plausible residual confounding would have reduced an observed effect, or would have produced an effect when none was observed (+1) |

Lesson 5 prints the large-effect line as “relative risk reduction ≤ 0.5” for +1 and “≤ 0.8” for +2, next to risk ratio ≥ 2 and ≥ 5. The risk-ratio cut-offs point toward larger effects. The “≤” on relative risk reduction points the other way. Name the three factors in the answer. If a number is required, use the risk-ratio cut-offs.

Certainty of evidence and strength of a recommendation are separate. A strong recommendation can rest on very low-certainty evidence, and a weak recommendation can rest on high-certainty evidence.

## 8. Methods and graphs in the quantitative analysis

Quantitative synthesis pools at least two sufficiently similar studies into a weighted average effect. That average is the meta-analysis. If pooling is not reasonable, the review stays qualitative.

**Effect measures**

| Data | Measures |
| --- | --- |
| Dichotomous | Odds ratio (OR), risk ratio (RR), risk difference |
| Continuous | Mean difference (MD), standardised mean difference (SMD) |

OR, RR, and SMD do not keep the original measurement unit. MD does.

**Models**

| | Fixed effect | Random effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assumption | One true effect. Observed differences are sampling error | True effects differ across studies and are distributed around a mean |
| Aim | Estimate that common effect | Estimate the mean of the distribution, for a range of settings |
| Weight | Within-study variance only. Large studies dominate | Within-study and between-study variance. Small studies receive relatively more weight |
| Interval | Narrower | Wider, because between-study variance is included |

Choose a fixed-effect model when the studies look functionally alike and the aim is one common effect. Choose random effects when heterogeneity is present and unexplained, or when the aim is to generalise across settings. Lesson 6 also allows a random-effects model when the Q test is not significant but the studies are not functionally equivalent.

**Estimators on the slides**

| Model | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed effect | Inverse variance, preferred when there are few studies and each is large. Mantel–Haenszel for sparse dichotomous data and many small studies. Peto for odds ratios, including a zero cell; it can seriously underestimate a large odds ratio. Maximum likelihood for large samples |
| Random effects | Inverse-variance weighted least squares. DerSimonian–Laird is the usual method-of-moments estimate of τ². Also maximum likelihood, restricted maximum likelihood, and the exact method of Van Houwelingen when normality does not hold |

In the RevMan table: dichotomous OR can use Mantel–Haenszel, inverse variance, or Peto under a fixed effect, and DerSimonian–Laird under random effects. Risk ratio uses Mantel–Haenszel or inverse variance. Risk difference and continuous outcomes use inverse variance. Random-effects analysis of continuous outcomes also uses inverse variance.

Weight by the inverse of the variance, not by sample size alone. A study of 100 participants is treated as a more precise estimate than a study of 10.

**When heterogeneity needs explaining**

- Sensitivity analysis: repeat the analysis under changed assumptions, for example after removing a low-quality study or switching model, and see whether the conclusion changes.
- Subgroup analysis: split on a pre-specified characteristic such as age, sex, setting, regimen, or design, and compare the size of effect.
- Meta-regression: the effect size is the dependent variable and study-level features are the predictors. The number of observations is the number of studies.

**Graphs**

| Graph | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Forest plot | Each study’s effect and confidence interval, its weight, and the pooled effect. The line of no effect is 0 for a mean difference and 1 for a ratio. Relative effects are drawn on a log scale. Overlapping intervals mean less heterogeneity. The plot also prints τ², χ², df, p, I², and the test of the overall effect |
| Funnel plot | Effect on the x-axis and precision or study size on the y-axis. Used mainly as a visual check for publication bias; it is also listed among the heterogeneity graphs |
| L’Abbé plot | Control event rate on x, experimental event rate on y. Point size reflects weight or sample size. A wide scatter away from the overall effect line suggests heterogeneity |
| Galbraith plot | Precision (1 / standard error) on x, standardised effect on y. Scatter around the regression line shows heterogeneity and outliers |
| Baujat plot | A study’s contribution to Cochran’s Q on x, and its influence on the pooled effect on y. Studies in the upper right are influential outliers |
| Bubble plot | Used with meta-regression. Bubble size reflects the study, and the line shows the covariate |

## 9. Cochrane’s position on the use of AI

The course states this position in the RAISE 2026 guidance. The recommendations were developed by a team led from the International Collaboration for Automation in Systematic Reviews, Cochrane, and Campbell. The position is that AI may be used, and Cochrane already uses it, but people remain accountable and the standards of a systematic review stay in place.

What Cochrane already does:

- Since 2016, Cochrane has used machine learning to identify randomized trials.
- The Cochrane RCT Classifier is two models together. It was trained on about 280,000 records checked by Cochrane Crowd, checked against the Clinical Hedges set, and validated on about 92,000 trials included in Cochrane reviews. Training records were not reused for testing.
- Information specialists, independently of the developers, required about **99% recall** before the classifier could be used in Cochrane reviews, because missing a relevant study is not acceptable.

Rules for anyone doing a review, including a Cochrane review:

1. The human authors are accountable for the content, methods, and findings, including the decision to use AI, how it is used, and what it changes.
2. Use of a tool has to be justified. The tool must be methodologically sound, must not undermine the trustworthiness of the review, and must fit that specific review. Its evaluations have to be read critically.
3. AI is not an author. It can assist writing. It cannot meet authorship criteria. Human authors answer for its use.
4. AI must not be used to fabricate or falsify data, results, or findings.
5. A chatbot is not a knowledge base. Do not rely on it for factual answers. Be aware that some papers in the search are themselves partly or wholly written by AI.
6. Report AI whenever it makes or suggests a judgement: eligibility, critical appraisal or risk of bias, data extraction, synthesis, certainty of evidence including GRADE, or text that states the strength of the evidence, the implications, or a plain-language summary.
7. The report names the tool, version, and date; the purpose and the steps affected; the justification and the validation; how outputs were checked by people; interests and funding; and limitations. PRISMA already asks authors to report automation tools. Inputs, outputs, data, and code are made public where that is practical.
8. Ethical, legal, and regulatory duties still apply: plagiarism, copyright, licensing, confidentiality, and privacy.
9. In screening, machine learning may rank records that are more likely to be relevant. A person still makes the decision, and the tool is validated. It works best after a training set.

The existence of an AI tool is not itself a reason to use it.

## 10. Heterogeneity: types and detection

Heterogeneity is variation among the studies in a systematic review. It is expected, because the studies were done in different settings. The questions are whether the studies differ, whether the difference can be measured, whether they should be pooled, and how.

**Three types**

| Type | What varies | Examples on the slides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clinical | Participants, interventions, or outcomes | Biological characteristics, type of intervention, outcomes assessed; also severity, setting, and dose or frequency |
| Methodological | How the studies were done | Research design and measurement tools, and the resulting difference in risk of bias |
| Statistical | The size of the effect | Effect estimates differ from one study to another by more than chance |

**Seeing it**

On a forest plot, point estimates that sit far apart and confidence intervals that do not overlap suggest heterogeneity. Greater overlap suggests less. Lesson 6 also uses four other plots: L’Abbé, Galbraith, Baujat, and the funnel plot. Their axes are in question 8.

**Measuring it**

| Statistic | What it is | How to read it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cochran’s Q | A chi-square statistic. Weighted sum of squared differences between each observed effect and the weighted average. Degrees of freedom = number of studies − 1 | The null hypothesis is that the studies are homogeneous. p < 0.05 indicates heterogeneity. p > 0.05 indicates homogeneity on this test. The test has low power when there are few studies, so it is read together with I² and τ² |
| I² | The percentage of observed variability in effects that comes from real differences among studies rather than chance. \(I^2 = 100\% \times (Q - df) / Q\). Range 0 to 100% | Higgins et al. 2003, as printed: 25% low, 50% moderate, 75% substantial. The same slide also prints the overlapping Cochrane bands: 0–40% might not be important, 30–60% moderate, 50–90% substantial, 75–100% considerable. A low I² argues against a subgroup analysis; a large I² is a reason to consider one. I² does not name the cause |
| τ² | The between-study variance of the true effects. It is estimated in the random-effects model | Closer to zero means little true variability. Larger values mean more. It is an absolute variance, not a percentage, and it is not interpreted on its own as “high” or “low” heterogeneity |

Q is large when studies scatter widely around the average effect. A large **p-value** means the opposite of a large Q: p > 0.05 is the homogeneous result.

**What to do when it is present**

Do not pool; use a fixed-effect model only when the studies really share one effect; or use a random-effects model and include the between-study variance. To look for reasons, use a pre-specified subgroup analysis, a sensitivity analysis, or meta-regression. Meta-regression uses one row per study, not one row per patient.
